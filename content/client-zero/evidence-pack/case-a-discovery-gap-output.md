# Discovery Gap Agent Output: Synthetic Case A  -  Qualified Discovery

> **Classification:** Clearly synthetic test material. Northstar Cyber Advisory, Maya Singh, Alex Romero, the two consultants, and every case fact referenced below are fictional. This output is a synthetic workflow-test artifact, not customer evidence, a testimonial, a case study, or a real commercial opportunity.

## Deal and workflow context

The fictional eighteen-person UK cybersecurity consultancy is considering a £5,000, two-week GenX AI Pro Starter Sprint covering two manually operated, human-reviewed workflows:

1. Turn discovery notes and approved historical material into a proposal and SOW first draft for Maya to review.
2. Turn consultants’ weekly updates into a standard client-status summary for Maya to review.

The intended change is narrower than end-to-end automation. The Sprint would reduce blank-page assembly and expose gaps, assumptions, exclusions, risks, and review points. Pricing, claims, scope, legal terms, delivery commitments, client decisions, and external sending remain under human control. The first phase excludes integrations, automatic sending, legal approval, certification, and custom application development.

- Weekly consultant notes vary in format; Alex manually consolidates them; Maya reviews risks and commitments before manual sending.
- Human review and manual external sending are mandatory.
- The proposed package, duration, price, two workflows, deliverables, acceptance criteria, exclusions, and decision process are stated.
- The fictional client does not want custom software or production integrations in phase one.
- Historical and sanitized examples are stated as available but have not been inspected.

### Assumptions supplied by the case, not yet verified

# 2. Impact of each gap

| Gap | Qualification impact if unresolved |
|---|---|
| Source-material readiness and quality | The team could sell a two-workflow Sprint and then discover that the examples are unusable, unrepresentative, or not approved. That threatens feasibility and the two-week delivery window. |
| Exact input and output boundaries | “A proposal draft” or “a status summary” could mean different things to Maya, Alex, and the builder, producing avoidable rework and subjective acceptance. |
| Evaluation method and acceptance sign-off | The stated criteria cannot reliably govern handoff unless all parties know how outputs will be tested, what counts as a material failure, and who signs off. |
| Approved AI environment and information handling | Build access could be blocked or sanitized material could be handled outside the fictional client’s approved boundary. The Sprint must not imply security or compliance approval. |
| Proposal variants and commercial guardrails | The workflow may overreach across unlike proposal types, or draft sensitive commercial language that should instead be blank, quoted from an approved source, or explicitly flagged. |
| Reporting taxonomy and escalation rules | Summaries may look consistent while still hiding risk because labels, severity, missing-update handling, and escalation presentation differ by reviewer. |
| Baseline pain and value test | Budget alignment is known, but commercial value remains weakly evidenced. The proposal can describe the problem and evaluation plan, but cannot credibly promise savings or ROI. |
| Operating model after handoff | Even a good agent pack may be inconsistently used or modified, weakening traceability and human-review controls after the seven-day support period. |
| Stakeholder validation | The proposed design may reflect only the sponsor’s view and miss the actual operator burden or consultant input constraints. |
| Kickoff readiness and scheduling | The Sprint may be commercially approved but unable to start because payment, materials, access, or reviewers are not ready. |
| Scope-change boundary | Requests for integrations or additional workflows could consume the fixed Sprint without an explicit change decision. |

# 6. Manager coaching notes

- **This opportunity is qualified around a real workflow shape, not around proven ROI.** Pain, ownership, review gates, manual operating mode, budget, duration, and two candidate workflows are stated. Preserve that strength without converting Maya’s six-to-eight-hour estimate into a savings claim.
- **Do not demo past the evidence.** A polished generic proposal generator would conceal the most important open question: whether Northstar’s stated examples support a repeatable, traceable draft process.
- **Make source inspection a delivery gate.** Because source files arrive after payment, use a pre-payment manifest and write a clear kickoff/rescope condition for unusable or materially different materials.
- **Sell the controlled workflow, not autonomous output.** The value proposition is consistent first drafts, visible gaps, and faster human review where achieved - not automatic commercial judgment or sending.
- **Protect Maya’s approval boundary.** Pricing, legal terms, claims, delivery commitments, external wording, client risk decisions, and sending remain human-controlled.
- **Give Alex operational authority without confusing it with final approval.** Alex should validate usability and own day-to-day operation; Maya should retain commercial and final acceptance authority unless the parties explicitly agree otherwise.
- **Turn acceptance criteria into a scored review.** Traceability, missing-evidence behavior, visible assumptions/exclusions/risks, and no automatic sending should each have observable test evidence.
- **Treat information-handling questions as client approval items.** Do not imply that this Sprint certifies the AI tool, privacy posture, security, or compliance.
- **Keep scope fixed.** If the examples expose multiple distinct proposal types or reporting processes, choose the bounded first variant or initiate a scope decision; do not absorb hidden workflows.
- **Coach toward a decision.** The next meeting should conclude with approve, conditional approve, or defer - not merely another exploration session.
